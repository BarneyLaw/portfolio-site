CS1231S opens your third eye and cements your belief in God.

That is obviously not one of the official learning outcomes, but it describes the experience rather well. Before this module, mathematics mostly meant calculating an answer. CS1231S introduced me to a different kind of mathematics: defining objects precisely, making claims about them, and proving why those claims must be true.

It was entirely new, often confusing and occasionally mind-blowing. It also became one of the modules that most clearly justified why a computer science degree contains so much mathematics.

## Content overview

The module begins with sets, propositions, predicates and logic. You learn to translate ordinary statements into formal ones, manipulate quantifiers, and pay attention to tiny differences in wording that completely change a claim. “For every” and “there exists” stop being decorative phrases. Their order matters, and reversing them can turn a true statement into nonsense.

From there, we moved into proof techniques: direct proofs, contradiction, contraposition and induction. Relations and functions provided the language for describing how objects connect, while graphs and trees turned that language into structures that appear throughout computer science. Counting, combinatorics and probability then gave us tools for reasoning about how many outcomes exist and how likely they are.

These topics sound like a miscellaneous basket of mathematics until you notice where they reappear. Logic underlies specifications and circuits. Induction mirrors recursive definitions. Graphs model networks, dependencies and state spaces. Counting matters whenever an algorithm must search possible configurations. The module is less about teaching one application than supplying the mathematical vocabulary used across the field.

## Proofs feel like discovering a new mechanic

The introduction of proofs was the part that changed how I saw mathematics. The classic proof that the square root of two is irrational was a particularly good first example. You assume the opposite, follow the consequences, and discover that the supposedly simplest fraction must have an impossible property. The conclusion does not come from collecting enough numerical evidence. It is forced by the structure of the argument.

Cantor's diagonal argument was even more memorable. The proof constructs an object that differs from every item in a proposed complete list, showing that some infinities are larger than others. Who looks at infinity, decides one version is insufficient, and then proves it with a diagonal? Mathematicians, apparently.

That cleverness is what I enjoyed most. A good proof can feel less like a long calculation and more like finding the one observation that makes an impossible puzzle collapse. It shows that mathematics has its own form of engineering elegance: the result is supported by a small structure in which every part has to hold.

Of course, “cannot be argued against” comes with an important qualification. A theorem is inevitable relative to its definitions, axioms and valid rules of inference. Mathematics is powerful not because statements descend from the sky, but because we make the assumptions explicit and then refuse to hand-wave the route to the conclusion.

## Where it becomes difficult

The same precision that makes proofs beautiful also makes them easy to get wrong. A proof can feel persuasive in English while hiding a missing case or an invalid implication. Once relations, functions, nested quantifiers and combinatorial arguments accumulate, it becomes possible to read a page several times and understand less after each attempt.

Tutorials matter here. Watching a polished solution is useful, but writing the proof yourself exposes whether you actually know why each step follows. The unpleasant truth is that familiarity is not the same as understanding. I often recognised the shape of a theorem while still being unable to produce a valid argument from scratch.

The examinations in my semester seemed more approachable than some past-year papers. That was welcome, although it also makes comparisons between cohorts risky. The teaching team and assessment style can change; a famously terrifying historical paper is not a guaranteed forecast.

## Verdict

CS1231S is a strong introduction to the mathematical foundations of computer science. It teaches tools you will meet again in algorithms, computability, networks, security and many other areas, but its value goes beyond immediate usefulness. It demonstrates that mathematics can be genuinely beautiful.

You may leave with more questions than answers and a suspicious new interest in infinity. That sounds like a recommendation to me.
