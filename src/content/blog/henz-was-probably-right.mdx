In my first year, in the first programming module I ever took, Martin Henz said something that I dismissed almost immediately: that programming languages are basically the true fundamentals and the essence of computer science. Two years later, about to head into third year, I have quietly come around to it. This is a writeup of how that happened, because the reconciliation turned out to be more interesting than either the original claim or my original skepticism.

## Why I did not believe him

At the time it sounded like the kind of thing a languages professor would say, especially one whose research focuses on programming languages. I was a first-year who thought computer science was the stuff that looked like computer science from the outside. Building apps. Making things that people use. All the stuff that gets talked about online as the "real" work, the visible surface where you ship a product and someone clicks a button and it does something.

Programming languages, by comparison, felt like a niche. An academic corner. Interesting maybe, but surely not the center of the whole field. The center was obviously the applications, the things you build with the languages, not the languages themselves. Saying languages were the essence felt like a compiler person insisting the compiler was the point when clearly the point was whatever you compiled.

So I filed it away as an overstatement and moved on.

## What changed over two years

Two things wore down that skepticism.

The first was just exploring the field. Over two years you touch a lot of domains, and after enough of them you start asking a different question than "what can I build." You start asking what is actually underneath all of it. And every path down eventually hits the same floor. The app runs on a runtime. The runtime is implemented in a language. That language was compiled or interpreted by something someone wrote. Peel back any layer far enough and you arrive at a compiler or an interpreter, and those are the artifacts that make every other layer possible. Nothing above them exists without them.

The second was the job market, honestly. Watching the layoffs, watching bootcamp grads ship apps, watching the general realization that a huge fraction of what gets called software engineering is now something almost anyone in the world can be trained to do in months, I started wanting to know what actually makes this a distinct discipline. What is the thing that is genuinely computer science and not just "typing instructions into a framework someone else built." If everyone can build the app, the app is not the essence. It cannot be the thing that defines the field, because the field predates all of it and will outlast whatever framework is fashionable this year.

## The thing that is actually load-bearing

Here is where I landed, and where I think Henz was right.

Without the compilers and interpreters that the giants before us built, we do not have a trade. Not a diminished trade, no trade. Every application, every framework, every bootcamp curriculum, every "anyone can be a SWE" pipeline sits on top of language implementations that someone had to design and build from an understanding of the machine that most people using them will never have. The applications are the visible part. The languages are the part that has to be true for the visible part to exist at all.

That reframes what "essence" means. It is not that building languages is the most common work, or the most lucrative, or the most visible. It is that it is the load-bearing part. If removing something collapses everything above it, that something was doing the real work and everything else was standing on it. Remove the apps and computer science survives fine. Remove the languages and compilers and there is nothing left to remove the apps from.

That is what I could not see as a first-year, because as a first-year you only ever see the top of the stack. You have to spend a while climbing down before you notice that the floor you have been standing on the whole time is the actual structure, and everything you thought was the building was decoration on top of it.

## What I take from it

I am not going to pretend I now think everyone should go build a language, or that application work is not real work. It is, and most of us will do it. But the question I actually cared about, what makes computer science unique as a discipline rather than a job anyone can be funneled into, has an answer I did not expect to find in a first-year lecture.

The unique thing is that we build the tools we think in. We design and implement the very languages that everything else is written in. That is not a corner of the field. It is the field, and the rest is what we do with it.

It took two years to understand one sentence. That is a slow return, but it is the kind of idea that compounds quietly, and I suspect I will keep understanding it more as I go. Henz said it in week one. I just needed the two years to know what he meant.

## Programming Language focus area?

Now will I perhaps pivot into the domain of programming languages? Probably not. I am still more interested in the applications and the infrastructure that runs them. But I will be looking at them with a different lens, and I will be thinking about the languages that make them possible, and the people who built those languages, and the fact that they are the ones who actually made this whole field possible.
